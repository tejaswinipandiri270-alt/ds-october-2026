Agile, Scrum and writing good tickets

How this programme runs as weekly sprints, what Scrum and Kanban actually say, and how to write tickets that someone else could pick up, finish and check without asking you a single question. Come back to this page whenever you plan a sprint or write a card.

1How we work here

Intuition. The programme is run like a small software team: work is broken into cards, the cards move across a board, and every week ends with something shown and reviewed.

The basics of the software development lifecycle (SDLC), Waterfall and Agile are covered on a separate page: SDLC, Waterfall and Agile. This page goes deeper into day-to-day practice: the rules of Scrum, the habits of Kanban, and the craft of writing a good ticket.

Our board

We use a Trello board. A board is a set of columns (Trello calls them lists), and each piece of work is a card (also called a ticket). Cards move left to right:

The house rules

RuleWhy
Each sprint lasts one week.A sprint is a fixed, short period in which a planned set of work is finished. Short sprints mean fast feedback.
Daily scrum at 09:15, when time permits.A short daily check-in so problems surface the same day, not at the end of the week (section 10).
Tuesday presentations act as the sprint review (from Week 2).You show what you finished and get feedback that shapes the next sprint (section 11).
You never move your own card to Done. Move it to Review; the trainer moves it to Done after checking it."Done" means "someone other than the author has checked it against the criteria". Self-certified work is not Done.
From Week 2 you write your own tickets.Breaking vague goals into clear, checkable pieces of work is a core professional skill, not admin.
Keep the board true. If you're working on it, it's in Doing. If you're stuck, say so on the card.The board is how the trainer (and later, your manager) sees progress without interrupting you.

Common mistake: dragging a card straight from Doing to Done "because it works on my machine". Move it to Review, add a comment saying what to look at (a link to the commit, notebook or slide), and let the reviewer decide.

2The Agile Manifesto in plain English

Intuition. Agile is not a process; it's a set of priorities. When you have to choose, prefer people, working results, collaboration and adapting over paperwork and rigid plans.

In 2001 seventeen software practitioners wrote the Manifesto for Agile Software Development. It has four values and twelve supporting principles. Each value is written as "this over that". The authors were explicit that the things on the right still matter; the things on the left simply matter more.

ValueIn plain EnglishWhat it looks like for a data scientist
Individuals and interactions over processes and toolsTalking to each other solves more problems than following a form or choosing the perfect tool.Ask a colleague about a strange column now, rather than writing a long ticket and waiting two days.
Working software over comprehensive documentationThe real measure of progress is something that runs and can be used, not a long document describing it.A baseline model that runs end to end beats a 10-page plan for a perfect model. (Still write a short README.)
Customer collaboration over contract negotiationWork with the people who will use the result throughout, rather than arguing about what was agreed at the start.Show the stakeholder early charts and ask "is this the question you meant?" before you tune anything.
Responding to change over following a planPlans are useful, but when you learn something new, change the plan.The data shows the target is leaking? Re-plan the sprint rather than finishing a model you now know is wrong.

A few of the twelve principles, paraphrased

  • Deliver working results frequently, from every couple of weeks to every couple of months, preferring the shorter timescale.
  • Welcome changing requirements, even late, if they give the customer an advantage.
  • Business people and developers should work together daily.
  • Face-to-face conversation is the most effective way to share information.
  • Sustainable pace: a team should be able to keep going indefinitely, not sprint itself into exhaustion.
  • Simplicity, the art of maximising the work not done, is essential.
  • At regular intervals the team reflects on how to become more effective, then adjusts.

Agile vs Scrum vs Kanban. Agile is the philosophy (the values and principles). Scrum and Kanban are two concrete ways of putting it into practice. Many teams mix them.

3Scrum: accountabilities, events, artefacts

Intuition. Scrum is a lightweight framework: a small team works in fixed-length sprints, plans at the start, checks in daily, shows the result at the end and then reflects on how to work better.

The official definition is the Scrum Guide (by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland; the current edition is from 2020). It is short, around 13 pages, and deliberately leaves a lot out. Popular practices such as user stories, story points and burndown charts are not part of the Scrum Guide; they are common additions that many Scrum teams use.

Scrum rests on three pillars: transparency (the work and the process are visible), inspection (look at progress often) and adaptation (change course when something is off). Every event below exists to inspect something and adapt.

The three accountabilities

A Scrum Team is small, typically 10 people or fewer, with no sub-teams or hierarchy. The 2020 Guide talks about accountabilities rather than "roles": each one is responsible for a different outcome.

AccountabilityResponsible forIs not
Product Owner (one person)Maximising the value of the product. Owns the Product Backlog: writes and explains items, orders them by priority, and sets the Product Goal.A committee. Others can suggest items, but one person decides the order.
Scrum MasterThe team's effectiveness. Teaches Scrum, facilitates events when needed, removes impediments (blockers), and helps the organisation work with the team.A project manager who assigns tasks.
DevelopersCreating a usable Increment each sprint. They make the plan for the sprint (Sprint Backlog), keep to the Definition of Done, adapt their plan daily, and hold each other accountable.Only programmers. Anyone doing the work counts: data scientists, analysts, testers, designers.

The five events

An event is a meeting with a purpose and a timebox (a maximum length). The maximums below are for a one-month sprint; shorter sprints usually have shorter events.

EventPurposeTimeboxOutput
The SprintThe container for all the other events. A fixed length; a new one starts as soon as the last ends.One month or lessA usable Increment
Sprint PlanningAnswers three questions: why is this sprint valuable (the Sprint Goal)? What can be done? How will it be done?Up to 8 hoursSprint Goal + Sprint Backlog
Daily ScrumDevelopers inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for the next day.15 minutesAn updated plan; blockers raised
Sprint ReviewShow the stakeholders what was achieved, discuss what changed, and decide what to do next. A working session, not just a demo.Up to 4 hoursA revised Product Backlog
Sprint RetrospectiveInspect how the team worked (people, tools, process, Definition of Done) and pick improvements.Up to 3 hoursOne or more improvements to try

Backlog refinement (sometimes called grooming) is an ongoing activity, not a formal event: breaking down, clarifying and estimating upcoming items so they're ready for a future Sprint Planning.

The three artefacts and their commitments

An artefact is a visible record of work or value. Each one has a commitment: the thing that gives it focus and lets you measure progress against it.

ArtefactWhat it isCommitmentCommitment in plain English
Product BacklogThe ordered list of everything that might be needed in the product. Never "finished".Product GoalThe longer-term objective the backlog works towards.
Sprint BacklogThe Sprint Goal, the items chosen for this sprint, and the plan for delivering them. Owned by the Developers.Sprint GoalThe single objective for this sprint. Scope can be renegotiated; the goal stays.
IncrementA concrete, usable step towards the Product Goal. Several can be made in one sprint.Definition of DoneThe quality bar every piece of work must meet before it counts as part of the Increment.

4Kanban basics and WIP limits

Intuition. Stop starting, start finishing. Make the work visible, cap how much is in progress at once, and keep it flowing to Done.

Kanban (Japanese for roughly "signboard" or "visual card") comes from Toyota's manufacturing system and was adapted for knowledge work. Unlike Scrum it has no sprints and no prescribed roles. Its core practices are:

  1. Visualise the work. Every item is a card on a board whose columns show the steps of your workflow.
  2. Limit work in progress (WIP). Each "in progress" column has a maximum number of cards. When it's full, you finish something before starting something new.
  3. Manage flow. Watch how smoothly cards move. A card that sits still is a signal: it's blocked, too big, or waiting on someone.
  4. Make policies explicit. Write down the rules, for example "a card enters Review only with a link to the work".
  5. Improve continuously, based on what the board shows you.

Why WIP limits work

Starting five things feels productive. In practice every switch between tasks costs time, nothing gets finished, and nobody can review half-done work. With a limit of 2:

  • work reaches Review sooner, so feedback arrives sooner;
  • blockers become obvious, because a stuck card is taking up one of only two slots;
  • you are pushed to help a teammate finish, rather than starting yet another thing.

Two flow measures are worth knowing. Cycle time is how long a card takes from Doing to Done. Throughput is how many cards reach Done per week. A well-known result from queueing theory (Little's Law) says that, on average, cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput: so if you take on more at once without finishing faster, each item takes longer.

Try it: a mini Kanban board with a WIP limit
Move cards with the arrows. Doing has a WIP limit of 2. In trainee mode you can take cards as far as Review; switch to trainer mode to accept them into Done or send them back.

Scrum vs Kanban

ScrumKanban

Our programme is a hybrid, sometimes called Scrumban: Scrum's weekly sprints and review, on a Kanban-style board with a WIP limit in Doing.

6Anatomy of a good ticket

Intuition. A good ticket answers four questions for a stranger: what is to be done, why, how will we know it's finished, and how big is it.

The test: could a teammate pick this card up tomorrow, while you're off sick, finish it, and could the reviewer check it, without either of them messaging you?

FieldWhat good looks likeExample
TitleStarts with a verb, specific, short (under about 70 characters). Readable on the board at a glance."Train logistic regression baseline on churn data"
DescriptionThe context and the why. Often a user story (below), plus links to data, notebooks or docs, and anything out of scope."As the trainer, I want a simple baseline so that later models can be compared against it."
Acceptance criteriaA short list of specific, testable conditions that must all be true for this ticket to be accepted."ROC-AUC and recall on the test set are logged in MLflow."
LabelsA few consistent tags so the board can be filtered.data-prep, modelling, mlops, presentation, bug
Assignee / memberExactly who is doing it. Unassigned cards in Doing are a red flag.You
Due dateOnly when there's a real deadline; otherwise the sprint end is the deadline.Tuesday, before the presentation
EstimateRelative size in story points or a T-shirt size.3 points
LinksTo the GitHub branch or pull request, the notebook, the MLflow run, related or blocking cards.github.com/…/pull/12
ChecklistThe small steps you'll take. Optional, but useful for your own tracking.Load → split → fit → evaluate → log
CommentsProgress updates, decisions, and blockers, with dates and @mentions."Blocked: no read access to the S3 bucket. @trainer could you grant it?"

User stories

A user story describes a need from the point of view of the person who benefits:

As a <type of user> I want <something> so that <benefit>.

  • As a retention manager, I want a weekly list of the customers most likely to churn so that my team can call them first.
  • As a data scientist on the team, I want every experiment logged in MLflow so that I can reproduce and compare runs.
  • As the trainer, I want a 10-minute presentation of the week's results so that I can give feedback before next sprint.

The "so that" part is the most valuable: it tells you what problem you're actually solving, which helps you make sensible decisions the ticket didn't spell out. Not every ticket needs to be a story; a plain technical task ("Pin package versions in requirements.txt") is fine as it is.

Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria (AC) are the conditions this particular ticket must meet to be accepted. Good AC are specific, testable (someone can say yes or no), and about the outcome rather than every step. Two common styles:

Checklist style

  • Data split 80/20, stratified on churn, random_state=42
  • Logistic regression trained inside a scikit-learn pipeline
  • Precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC reported on the test set
  • Run logged in MLflow with parameters and metrics
  • Notebook runs top to bottom without errors

Given / When / Then style

Given the cleaned churn CSV in data/processed/
When I run python train.py --model logreg
Then a new MLflow run appears with ROC-AUC and recall logged
And the model file is saved to models/.

Comes from behaviour-driven development (BDD). Good when there's a clear trigger and observable result.

Data science twist: don't make the result the criterion. "ROC-AUC ≥ 0.85" is risky as an acceptance criterion, because you can't know in advance what the data allows. Make the AC about doing the work properly and recording the outcome: "compare 3 models with 5-fold CV; log ROC-AUC for each; write a one-paragraph conclusion". A target can live in the description as an aim.

Words that make AC untestable: good, better, nice, properly, clean, fast, works on their own. Replace them with something you could check: "under 2 seconds", "no missing values in columns X and Y", "runs on a fresh clone".

Definition of Done vs acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteriaDefinition of Done (DoD)
Applies toOne specific ticketEvery ticket
Written byWhoever writes the ticket (agreed with the Product Owner)The team / organisation, once
Answers"Did we build the right thing?""Is it built to our quality standard?"
Example"Recall on the churn class is reported""Code pushed to GitHub, README updated, reviewed by the trainer"

A ticket is Done only when both its own acceptance criteria and the shared DoD are met. A sensible DoD for this programme:

  • All acceptance criteria met.
  • Code and notebooks committed and pushed to GitHub; no secrets or .env files committed.
  • Notebook or script runs top to bottom on a fresh clone.
  • README or card comment explains how to run it and what was found.
  • Card moved to Review with a link to the work, and accepted by the trainer.

Estimates: story points and T-shirt sizes

An estimate is a rough measure of how big a ticket is, used to decide how much fits in a sprint. Agile teams usually estimate in relative terms: "this is about twice the size of that", rather than in hours. People are bad at absolute time estimates but quite good at comparing.

Story points combine effort, complexity and uncertainty into one number. Teams typically use a Fibonacci-like scale:

PointsT-shirtMeaningData science example
1XSTrivial, well understoodAdd .env to .gitignore
2SSmall, clearSet up a conda environment from a given file
3MA solid chunk; a few stepsTrain and evaluate a baseline model
5LBig, some unknownsCompare three models with cross-validation and tuning
8XLVery big or very uncertain; probably split itBuild a full feature-engineering pipeline for a new dataset
13+XXLToo big for one ticket. Split it."Build the churn model"
?Too unknown to sizeDo a time-boxed spike first (below)

The gaps widen as numbers grow because uncertainty grows with size: there's no point arguing whether something is a 9 or a 10. A common way to agree is planning poker: everyone reveals a number at once, and the highest and lowest explain their thinking.

  • Points are team-specific. Never compare one team's points with another's.
  • Velocity is the number of points a team usually completes per sprint; it helps plan the next sprint, not rate people.
  • A spike is a time-boxed investigation ("spend at most half a day finding out whether the API gives us daily data") whose output is knowledge, so a later ticket can be estimated.

7INVEST: a checklist for good tickets

Intuition. Six letters to check a ticket is ready to be worked on: it stands alone, can be discussed, is worth doing, can be sized, is small, and can be checked.

LetterMeansAsk yourselfFails if…
IIndependentCan it be done without waiting for another ticket?"Train model" and "Clean data" are one ticket's halves
NNegotiableIs it a goal to discuss, rather than a fixed contract of every detail?It dictates every function name before anyone has looked at the data
VValuableDoes someone benefit when it's done?"Refactor stuff" with no reason
EEstimableDo we understand it well enough to size it?"Use the new data" when nobody has seen the data (do a spike first)
SSmallCan it be finished within a sprint, ideally in a few days?Estimated at 13 points
TTestableCan a reviewer say yes or no to each criterion?"Model is good"

INVEST was coined by Bill Wake in 2003 for user stories, but it works for any ticket.

8Splitting big tickets

Intuition. If a ticket can't be finished in a few days, or can't be described in one sentence, it's really several tickets. Split it so each piece is still useful on its own.

Signs a ticket is too big: the estimate is 8 or more, the acceptance criteria run past seven or eight lines, the title contains "and", or it has been in Doing for days with no visible progress.

Split by…Big ticketSmaller tickets
Workflow stepBuild churn modelClean and split data · Train baseline · Compare models · Log to MLflow
Data sourceIngest all customer dataIngest billing CSV · Ingest support tickets API · Join on customer ID
Simple first, then extrasBuild a dashboard with filters, export and drill-downStatic dashboard with 3 key charts · Add date filter · Add CSV export
Happy path vs edge casesHandle all data quality issuesHandle missing values · Handle duplicates · Handle outliers in amount
Research vs buildUse an LLM to classify ticketsSpike: test 2 prompts on 50 examples (time-box 4h) · Build the classifier based on the spike
Model familyTry all the modelsTree-based models · Linear models · Compare and choose

Don't split by layer only (e.g. "write all the functions", then "write all the tests", then "write all the docs"). None of those pieces is useful until the last one is done. Prefer vertical slices: each piece takes a small thing all the way to a working, checkable result.

    10The daily scrum

    Intuition. Fifteen minutes to answer one question as a team: are we on track for this sprint's goal, and if not, what do we change today?

    Ours is at 09:15, when time permits. It's often called a stand-up because standing keeps it short. A popular format is three questions per person:

    The three questions

    1. Yesterday: what did I finish (or move forward) towards the sprint goal?
    2. Today: what will I work on?
    3. Blockers: is anything stopping me?

    A good update (about 30 seconds)

    "Yesterday I finished cleaning the churn data; it's in Review. Today I'm training the logistic regression baseline. Blocker: my MLflow runs aren't showing in the UI; I've added the details to the card. Could someone who has it working pair with me for ten minutes after this?"

    The 2020 Scrum Guide no longer prescribes these three questions; the Developers can use any format that focuses on progress towards the Sprint Goal. They remain a sensible default. Another option is to "walk the board" from right to left: discuss the cards nearest to Done first, because finishing them delivers value soonest.

    Good habits

    • Update your cards before the stand-up
    • Talk about cards and the goal, not about being busy
    • Raise blockers early; a blocker found on Monday is cheap
    • Agree to take detailed discussions offline ("let's talk after")
    • Offer help when someone else is stuck
    • Start on time, keep it to 15 minutes

    Anti-patterns

    • A status report to the trainer instead of the team
    • "Same as yesterday" for three days running
    • Problem-solving in the meeting while everyone waits
    • Hiding a blocker because it feels like failure
    • Cards on the board that don't match what people say

    Writing a blocker on the card: say what's blocked, what you've tried, and who can help. "Blocked since Wed 10:00: pip install lightgbm fails on M1 with a libomp error. Tried brew install libomp; same error. @trainer any known fix?" Then mention it at the next daily scrum.

    11Sprint review vs sprint retrospective

    Intuition. The review looks at what you built. The retrospective looks at how you worked.

    Sprint ReviewSprint Retrospective
    FocusThe product: the IncrementThe process: people, tools, ways of working
    WhoScrum Team + stakeholdersScrum Team only (a safe space)
    Key question"What did we achieve, and what should we do next?""How can we work better next sprint?"
    OutputFeedback; an updated Product BacklogOne or two concrete improvements to try
    HereTuesday presentations (from Week 2)A short reflection after the review, or your own

    Giving a good sprint review presentation

    • Start with the Sprint Goal and whether you met it.
    • Show working results (a chart, a run, a demo), not a list of tasks.
    • Be honest about what isn't done and why.
    • End with a question: what feedback do you need for next sprint?

    Start / Stop / Continue

    The simplest retrospective format. Everyone writes notes under three headings, the team groups them, and picks one or two actions (more rarely get done).

    Start

    "Writing acceptance criteria before starting a card." "Pushing to GitHub at the end of every day."

    Stop

    "Having four cards in Doing." "Tuning hyperparameters before there's a baseline."

    Continue

    "Pairing on environment problems." "Logging every run in MLflow."

    Other formats

    Mad / Sad / Glad (feelings), 4Ls (Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for), or simply "what went well / what didn't / what will we try".

    Make actions concrete: not "communicate better" but "post a blocker comment on the card within an hour of getting stuck". Check at the next retro whether it happened.
